The finding in one line. Difficulty, errors and paywalls do not separate the players who leave from the players who stay. Length of the first visit does. At the same amount of footage the two groups are indistinguishable on every screen measure we can count — so the lever is not a screen in the client, it is whatever gives a short first visit something to come back to.
01

Difficulty is not what removes anyone

Every one of the 361 records was read end to end and the player's state classified at the point the footage stops. Held at the same exposure, the two groups lose at the same rate.

Raw totals look dramatic and mean nothing. Unmatched, “hit a wall” reads 37% churned against 70% active — that entire gap is the six-fold difference in footage, and it closes to 53%/59% and 84%/82% inside matched bands.

What differs is not how often they lose. It is how many fights they have at all. Churned players make a median 24 boss attempts against the active group's 135.5, at a similar rate per hour (2.65 against 3.00). They simply have far fewer hours.

And the players who never lost did not breeze the game — they left before it could test them. Of the 35 churned players with zero losses in their whole record, 13 never fought a boss at all (median 12 recorded minutes, highest guide quest 1) and the other 22 fought a median of five, reaching guide quest 8.5. The comparable active figure is 6 of 74.

This is a finding, not a gap, and the control group proves it. Nine of the fourteen still-playing records came back as “cannot assess — the file ends before the player did”. The readers can tell a player stopping from a recorder stopping. When they say nothing explains it for someone who did stop, they looked and there was nothing there.

The 48 who came back look like the players who stayed on every screen measure. The 55 who came once are the group the first visit can still reach — and their visit contained nothing that went wrong: a median 42 minutes reaching guide quest 22 and level 14, in which 30 of 55 never lost a fight, 4 of 55 ever attempted a payment, and 20 of 55 touched anything social. How that single visit ends, read one at a time in a separate 155-player pass covering its own 45 one-day players: 8 progressing with no failure · 9 failed and recovered · 9 failed and parked in sleep mode · 3 idle · 7 on the exit or logout dialog · 5 barely started · 4 already an existing player's account. One reached guide quest 62 in a single 90-minute sitting and the recording ends in sleep mode. One went Settings → Account → “Would you like to log out?” on guide quest 29. One logged out 36 seconds into play.

They are not progressing more slowly. They are doing less while the client runs. In an idle game that is the distinction that matters: the game advances either way, so low input is not low progress, but it is low investment.

07

Why a short first visit has nothing to come back to

Every hook is gated behind a defeat

The Growth Guide, the first priced offer and the first real gear decision all fire within seconds of a loss. 35 of 103 churned players never lost a single fight in their whole record, against 6 of 74 active. Among churned players with 1–5 videos, 33 of 46 had zero fails in the first hour and 28 of those 33 saw no offer at all.

The machine works when it fires: a boss loss on TIME OVER, and “Welcome Epic Weapon” appears seven seconds later. The counter-case in full: one player's entire 14.9-minute record is nine attempts, nine wins, zero fails, and no offer at any point.

→ Fire the Growth Guide and the starter offer on a timer or on first-boss-entry, not only on a defeat. For roughly a third of new players these hooks currently never fire at all.

Every social surface is gated past the point these players stop

Observed unlock positions: dungeon 35 · world boss 59 · nickname 72 · friends list 85 · companion 96 · guild 141 · co-op party quests ~167 · arena 248. The one-day churned group's median highest guide quest is 22.

The first visit is pre-social by construction. This also explains the guild and companion gaps elsewhere in the pilot: most of the churned group never reached the unlock, so those are reach gaps, not preference gaps.

→ Bring one lightweight social touch inside the first 40 minutes.

09

The clock kills, and the screen never says so

46% of all defeats are TIME OVER, and in 45% of defeats the player was at or near full health when the fight ended. They were not dying. They ran out of time.

The gap that matters: when a player who later stopped recording times out, the boss still has a median 23% of its health left. For the players still being recorded it is 11%. They are twice as far from winning as they look, and nothing on screen tells them that, or by how much. The extreme cases are not close fights at all: the boss finishes at 95%, 91%, 87%, 75%.

→ On TIME OVER, print “not enough damage — the boss finished at X%” and put the cheapest upgrade that closes the gap on the same screen.

14

Limits, and where our own passes disagreed

What this data cannot say

A recording ending is our recorder stopping, not a player quitting. Many final videos hit the 900-second capture cap with the client still running. Footage covers roughly 40% of total play time, and the last recorded video is not necessarily the player's last session.

Guide-quest ids are a content index, not a progress scale. Ids jump by 17 in five seconds after one bulk summon, on-screen quest text contradicts the reference chain in 17 tabulated cases, and only about 195 of 657 observed quests are true milestones. No quest-level funnel can be drawn on them, by us or by you.

Two traps for any first-session metric you build. Dwell at a wall is 5–45× the time spent fighting it — one player logged 46 seconds of combat across two attempts against roughly 34 minutes elapsed at the same stage — so any stage-enter to stage-clear dwell metric measures the grind, not the fight. And 20 of 155 players reroll or class-swap, so any count keyed on “level 1 at stage 1-1” counts the same person more than once.

Figures we withdrew

This page supersedes four earlier internal passes. Three numbers from them should not be used:

WithdrawnWhy
“Churned players fail combat twice as often per video” (0.81 against 0.36)An artefact of counting whole records instead of matched exposure. At matched exposure the rate is 27.0% against 28.3%.
“141 players reached checkout, 47 confirmed”The tag counted also fires on free rewarded-ad claims and in-game gem spends, so it does not measure real-money intent.
“0 churned players ended stuck at a wall”The field flips between readers — 0 of 103 by census against 4 of 81 in a blind read. The wall rate is the robust number; this one is not.

What would settle the rest

Your numbered FTUE events per NPSN answers tutorial-step completion outright and calibrates every position claim here against your own ruler. An ADID to NPSN mapping turns two parallel evidence streams into one joined dataset. And the established-player benchmark — level 51+ at first sighting with 15+ recorded days, roughly 1,336 candidates already in this corpus — is the only way to say what a settled player's day looks like against a new player's. It is the most useful thing to run next.
